A crew of robots runs your desk.
Here's who they are.

Each one has a name, a shift, and one job. Start with a text message and follow it through the building — then meet the whole crew.

August 20 (late night): the complaints inbox went from 269 to 207 in one sitting — and the two biggest complaint factories were shut off at the source. The robots' complaints box had grown so big (269 unread) that nobody could face it. Read all together, the 232 robot-mistake notes turned out to be about ten problems, not two hundred. The biggest: forty-two notes were the same story — one robot says "not my job", the other never picks it up, and a customer waits. New house rule, written into the manual: see the same job twice and it is YOURS, no excuses — and if you truly cannot do it, you must file a to-do with a name on it, not another complaint. Second biggest: two dozen notes saying "robot X hasn't run in a while" that were true for an hour and then stale forever — those now clean themselves up the moment the robot checks back in, and 18 were swept out tonight. Separately, the new booking sign-in robot was caught queueing empty confirmation texts — and the empty text was hiding a worse fault: it couldn't see that a person had already been sent their confirmation by hand, so all four of its queued texts were doubles. Fixed, pushed live, and the four doubles cancelled before anyone could send them. The wording of confirmations also got Junyan's new ruling: one friendly line about rebooking (24 hours' notice, movable up to a month out) for everybody, not repeated within 90 days.

Earlier (night): it is live — and four real passes later it has not made a single duplicate card. The new way of finding people went onto the server and is now running on its own every half hour. Before letting it run unwatched we made it do a practice pass over the real board: 162 bookings, everybody found, nothing invented, and exactly one person flagged — alice hamman, who had two cards. We looked into why, and it was not what we assumed: one card was made by the desk at 3am, and the second was made by Junyan himself at 2:25pm, by hand, while setting up her door code. Not a robot fault at all — her card already existed and there was nothing to say so. The two cards are now merged into one (Junyan's, because it holds her door code and her booking), and the practice pass since shows nothing flagged and nothing to clean up at all.

The number that actually proves it: the card list has not grown by one. Still 1,051 cards, same as before. The old way quietly made a brand-new card every time a returning guest turned up on a fresh throwaway address — that is the whole problem — and four live passes in, the new way has made none.

One honest note: the push to the server had already been done earlier in the evening by another session working in parallel. So what we did tonight was the half that makes a push trustworthy — actually reading the result. A push nobody checked and a push nobody made look exactly the same from outside.

August 20 (later): a person can now be found by ANY address or number we have ever had for them — not just the one on their card. This was the fix behind the duplicate-cards problem, and it is built. Every way to reach somebody — their real email, their phone, and every throwaway line a booking site ever gave them — now lives on its own line in one table, tied to the person. We copied in everything we already held: 1,709 lines across all 1,051 cards, checked twice by two different methods because a single check on this system has lied to us before. Nothing had to be guessed: every phone number and address came out clean, including one UK number that had been unreadable because of invisible characters hiding inside it.

Then we proved it on the real board rather than on a test. Quynh Tran — one of three people whose second address was written down five days ago in a box nothing ever searched — is now found by her normal email and by her dead Peerspace line, and both land on the same card. A practice run over 162 real bookings found everybody, invented nothing, and flagged exactly one person: alice hamman, who genuinely has two cards sharing one email. That is a merge, and merges stay a human job.

Two smaller things came with it. A Giggster text now files its own line automatically, keyed to the conversation rather than the number, but deliberately with no person attached — a name like "Hanano I." is not proof of who someone is, so a human links it. And the Known Emails box is retired and renamed, because it was never searched by anything and writing in it only felt like fixing something.

One step is left and it is Junyan's: pushing the new version to the server. Until that happens the table is right but the live robot is still matching one address, the old way.

August 20 (night): we found out how long the booking sites' throwaway contact lines really last — by reading four months of our own mail, without texting a soul. This was the one thing we refused to guess at earlier in the day. We had assumed each made-up address belongs to one booking and dies with it. Both email sites turned out to work differently, in opposite directions. Peerspace hands out a new address more often than once per booking — one guest was given two seventeen minutes apart — and those addresses then keep working for a month after the booking is over; one is still alive 33 days on. Tagvenue makes a fresh one every time somebody merely asks about a space, whether or not they ever book: one guest has three, another four.

The good news is bigger than it sounds. Neither email site can ever hand an old address to a different person — Peerspace stamps the time into the address and Tagvenue stamps a number that is never reused — so an old line always points back at the right human. That shrinks the whole worry down to Giggster, whose text-message numbers say nothing about who is on the other end and come from a small pool.

And on Giggster we found the sharp thing. Its texts arrive from two kinds of number that look alike: real guests, and Giggster's own robot sending booking notices, cancellations and even our own login code — about different guests, all from one number. Save one of those as somebody's phone number and you have stapled Giggster the company onto a dancer's card. There is also a quiet trap underneath all of it: text a line that has gone dead and nothing bounces — not one bounce in four months — so the message just vanishes and it looks exactly like the guest ignoring us. Every rule we wrote therefore goes by age, never by waiting for an error.

One thing we deliberately did not do: text an old Giggster number to see what happened. Texting a number that might already belong to a stranger is the accident we are trying to prevent. So instead there is a new job that simply watches and shouts the day one number turns up with a second guest behind it. Also spotted on the way: Tagvenue warns that forwarding one of its emails can let the reader into our account — so we save the address, and never forward the mail.

August 20 (evening): decided — every person gets ONE card, with a list of ways to reach them attached. Booking sites like Peerspace and Giggster hand out made-up, throwaway email addresses and phone numbers for talking to guests. We had been saving those on people's cards as if they were real addresses, so every repeat booking minted a brand-new card for the same human — 42 cards sit on a throwaway address today, and one woman has four cards. Junyan already ruled the principle on August 15 (one card per person, the name is who you are); tonight's session settled the shape. A new companion table in Notion lists each person's contact entries: what it is (email or phone), where it came from (their real one, or one a platform made up), and — the discovery of the session — which booking it belongs to, because a throwaway line is really a temporary phone line the platform set up for one booking's conversation, not the person's address. The sending rule, in Junyan's words: we generally stay away from the throwaway lines, but if someone contacts us through one, we answer on that same line to keep the thread clean — real email and real phone for everything else. Old throwaway lines are never deleted: when a message arrives from one we've seen before, that is how we recognize "oh, this is Prajakta again" instead of making a fifth card. The Known Emails box — which it turns out nothing ever searched — retires into the new table. Duplicate cards get merge suggestions only; a human does every merge. And one thing we refused to guess: how long each platform actually keeps a throwaway line alive differs by platform (Giggster even reuses phone numbers from a pool, so a dead one could reach a stranger) — that became its own research ticket. The building job is now written up too: make the table, copy in every email and phone we already hold on all 1,040 cards, and change the booking robot from looking for one exact address to looking for any address or number we have ever seen for that person — which is the single change that stops the duplicates. It only adds; nothing is merged or deleted, and joining duplicate cards stays a separate job that a person does one at a time.

August 19 (late): the big busywork ticket was split up — one chore is done, five got their own tickets. Back in August we listed the chores the main thinking robot should never have been doing, and put them all on one ticket. Only the first one got finished: watching the alarm panels, live since August 16, checking every minute and calling staff itself. Switching that on taught us three lessons worth keeping. One wrong word in the deploy command wiped twelve settings and took the whole system down for four and a half hours — we read every message from that window one by one, and nothing urgent came in. A saved password had an invisible line-break on the end that the program refused, silently breaking 267 panel checks in a row. And when the alarm program tried to report its own failure, the report itself failed, because "Alarm Watch" wasn't on the list of things allowed to complain: the chore broke and its cry for help broke.

Meanwhile the ticket kept growing. Junyan's August 15 ruling — that all four chronically-dropped chores genuinely matter and none can be deleted — piled two more onto it, and it became too big for anyone to finish in one sitting. Junyan ruled: split it. Each remaining chore is now its own ticket you can follow on its own — the who's-in-today list, copying claimed staff shifts to the calendar, deleting the one leftover chore outright, the reconciling chore, and noticing when staff finish something. The idea they all share, in one line: programs find the work, the thinking robot only does work that got flagged. The robot runs out of time because hunting for jobs and doing jobs come out of the same budget. Move the hunting to a program and it can only ever fall behind on doing — and a growing pile of flagged work is visible in a way a skipped search never was.

Next up is the shift-copying one, which matters most: on August 14 three renters were told nobody would be there to help them, for bookings a staff member had already claimed.

August 19: we read all 9,465 messages the studio has ever sent — and found that some customers got the same text twice. Two leftover jobs were cleared off an old ticket, and neither was what we thought. The first was a tool meant to compare a robot’s rough draft with the final message, to teach it to write better. That tool was scrapped back in July because robots kept filling in the draft box and leaving the real box empty — so some messages went out blank. Junyan ruled: leave it dead. The new suggest-and-rewrite way of working already teaches the same lesson, in a better place.

The second job was a checkup hunting for messages accidentally written down twice. The old plan for it was junk: it only asked “same words?”, so every weekly reminder text looked like a mistake — about 500 false alarms, the same disease as the bad alarms we spent July untangling. So we read the whole record instead. Every message, all 9,465 of them. The good news is that our record book is clean: nothing was written down twice, and the way it saves messages makes doubles genuinely impossible — checked properly this time, not assumed.

The bad news is real and nobody knew. On June 21, three renters each got the same long message twice at 12:01, then twice again at 13:00 — four copies each within an hour. Two more people got doubled texts in May, and one person’s reply was recorded twice in August. Nothing is watching for this: the outbox shows one row per message, so a double send looks perfectly normal from there. Two new jobs followed — a checkup that hunts for double sends (starting in trial mode, where a person marks each finding real-or-noise before it may bother anyone), and a separate hunt for what caused June 21. Junyan closed the June 21 hunt the same day: old news, already dealt with. We are recording plainly that nobody went and re-checked it — that rests on Junyan remembering the fix rather than on anyone reading the records. It is a fair trade here only because the new checkup catches the same thing within minutes if it ever returns, and the evidence stays saved so we would not be starting from nothing.

One lesson worth keeping: a search that doesn’t say how much it actually looked at is worthless. Our own first search read 346 of the 9,465 messages — 4 percent — and cheerfully reported everything was fine. It happened a second time, a different way, before we caught it. That is the same trick the dead safety checker played in July when it kept showing a five-day-old page as if it were today’s. So the new checkup has to say out loud how much it read, every time it runs.

August 18: the program that sets up new bookings by itself is written and tested — and switched off on purpose, waiting for Junyan. When somebody books a studio directly, a handful of small chores follow: make them a profile, give them a working door code, put a card on the board two days before, get a confirmation ready. Until now those chores rode on the thinking robot, which means they waited for its next pass and sometimes got skipped when the pass ran out of room. This is a plain program instead — no AI — and it does something slightly unusual: it keeps no memory of which bookings it already handled. Every half hour it simply asks "what is missing?" and fills the gaps. That means it cannot lose its place, cannot restart into a mess, and cannot do the same thing twice. A memory has to be right forever; asking the question is right every single time.

The care went into what it refuses to do. Two people with the same name, or a door code on file that disagrees with the panel: it stops and writes on Junyan's list rather than guessing — both of those have burned us before, and picking a side is how somebody gets sent the wrong code. If it cannot read the booking calendar at all it says so out loud, because a broken read used to look exactly like a quiet day. And bookings happening today or tomorrow it does not touch at all — a different, faster robot already owns those, and two programs writing door codes for one booking is precisely the collision we had before. It still checks them, and if that faster one has failed, it writes that on the list instead of barging in.

It is not running. It writes real door codes, so switching it on is Junyan's to do, in this order: put it on the server with the off-switch held down, run one practice pass that only looks and reports, read what it says it would do against the real calendar, and only then let it go every half hour. One related bug got written up beside it: empty leftover door-code entries under a person's name currently make the system say "I cannot tell which of these is you" and block a renter whose code is perfectly fine — it happened to Hanano and to Hannah. The fix is to ignore anything with no code on it, since an empty entry opens no door.

August 15 (midday): the new robot ran four times and wrote its first two suggestions on its own — and lost one to nobody reading it. This was the one thing to watch for, and it happened on the very first run: a suggestion on the list written by a robot rather than a person. It spotted a 15-minute gap in Studio 527 between two of Kateryna's bookings, worked out that the tidy-looking fix (stretching the later booking) could quietly reshape every Sunday since June because that booking repeats, proposed a separate small booking instead, and deliberately left the money out of it. Then it checked again an hour later to make sure the gap was still there. The gap came and went at 10:30 with nobody having read the suggestion.

Nothing went wrong, and that is the interesting part. It refused to act without a yes — even with the clock running out, which is exactly the case the rule was written for. It refused to nag with a second copy. It refused to tick off its own work. All three rules held and the job was still missed. The bigger news is quieter: across all four runs it carried out nothing at all, because nothing has been approved yet. August 15: the back-and-forth was half-built for nine days, and the missing half was the robot itself. Junyan brought in yesterday's report from the main robot: seven passes, and on nearly every one the same small chores pushed to "next time" — the evening block never ran at all, the end-of-day booking review was simply lost for the day, a real alarm at 11:19am went unseen until lunchtime, and forty-odd messages sat unmatched going back to Monday. Same shape as the missing thank-you messages: the work was done, every note was honest, and the finishing half simply wasn't there. It is now its own job on the list to fix.

August 7 (night): the text collector does not "fail one run in four" — it had one bad day, and we found the real reason. We said for a while that the job collecting renters' texts failed about one run in four. Reading the actual record of the last hundred runs, that is not what happened. It ran fine, then failed on every single try for a whole day (from the evening of August 5 through the next night), then went green again and has stayed green for thirty-eight runs in a row. The scheduled times were never missed — the job started every half hour as it should. Three separate things caused that one bad day: a page in the manuals was missing a line that a checker insists on, so the checker stopped the whole job; GitHub itself had an outage that killed the job before any of our own code ran; and — the one that actually lost renters' texts — the collector was never told what name to sign its own work with, so whenever two runs tried to save at the same moment the save failed, it retried the same broken save six times, and the texts it had just collected were thrown away. That is why a text could vanish and the robots would see silence instead of a renter waiting. It is fixed, and tested. Nothing had to be re-collected: David's Wednesday-night texts are all in the log already, because the collector never marks a text as "done" until the save really works. One more fix went in beside it: when this job fails it now writes its own row on Junyan's list saying so, instead of failing quietly — a silent gap used to look exactly like a quiet evening. Still to do next week: watch a full clean week, and make it fail on purpose once to check that new row really appears.

August 6 (evening): the little sweep is built — it notices what already happened. Every 10 minutes it reads the list of suggested next steps and checks each one against the real booking. If the booking was already moved — by anyone, including Junyan doing it himself — the step ticks off on its own, marked "done by a person," and nobody is asked to write anything up. That was the whole complaint: he's faster doing it himself, and then owes the system a report. Not anymore. Two deliberate limits: the sweep cannot change a booking, only look at one (one robot does the work, a different one checks it — a checker that can also change things isn't a checker), and if it can't reach the booking system it says "couldn't check" rather than assuming the booking vanished. That second one matters: without it, one expired password would have wiped every open step off the board in a single sweep — the exact way the safety checker fooled us for five days in July. Four of the eighteen new tests exist only to keep that from ever happening. Junyan switches it on in the cloud when ready; there's a practice mode that reports what it would do and changes nothing.

August 6: three renters wrote to us and no robot answered — the quiet failure gets a watcher. A message lands in the log, one robot decides it's not its job, the next robot never picks it up, and nothing anywhere turns red. Shauna, Himanshi and David all waited like this in one week; Junyan caught each one himself. The rot isn't that a robot broke — it's that saying no leaves no mark, so every dashboard stays green while a renter waits. More instructions won't fix it; we tried that three times and the instructions got skipped along with everything else. Instead: every message must end somewhere — handled, ticketed, or a robot's name on the 'no' — and a separate watcher outside all the robots rings when a message sits too long, naming the person waiting. Two repair jobs were found tangled up with it: the message log writes doubles (18 batches in one day, the copier's fault), and the text collector fails one run in four — when it does, the robots see silence and think the renter never wrote.

August 5 (later that night): the conversation now has a place to happen. The two new lists exist in Notion: ⏭️ Next Action (one suggested step per ticket, with the proof beside it, waiting for a yes or a rewrite) and 🎚️ Action Classes (the scoreboard that tracks which kinds of step have earned the right to run on their own — charging money, sending someone out, and making door codes never earn it). The rulebook is written into the robots' own manual. We also checked exactly what the booking system lets a robot do: move, create, cancel and approve bookings, yes — change a price, no. So anything touching money always splits off and waits for a person. Two calls from Junyan finished the design: the old to-do board gets worked down to zero and then retired (no copying rows across), and instant bookings and moves run on their own from day one — with a text to Junyan each time one happens, so he sees it without being asked. Still to come: the little sweep that runs every few minutes, and then a week of real use with Junyan checking every step.

August 5 (night): the main robot's job list was scrapped. It stops being a to-do list and becomes a conversation. We sat down and went through its seven chores one at a time, and almost none of them belonged to a robot that thinks. Waiting two hours to notice an alarm is silly — that becomes a small program checking every minute and calling staff itself. The who's-in-the-studio-today list and the copying of claimed staff shifts are just copying: small programs too. One chore turned out to be done twice and gets deleted. What's left of the mail job shrinks to one small thing: open the message, start a ticket, pick the right procedure, stop.

Everything after that becomes a back-and-forth. The robot proposes ONE next step and shows the evidence behind it. Junyan either says yes, or rewrites the step to what it should have said, and marks it ready. Only then does the robot do it — and afterwards it looks at the real booking or calendar again to check it truly happened. Then it proposes the next step, until the ticket is finished. The rewriting is the whole point: the difference between what the robot suggested and what Junyan wrote is the lesson. Today when Junyan fixes something himself the correction vanishes; this catches it every time. It happened again this week — Dana's booking message went out missing the invoice line and the payment deadline, Junyan wrote it himself, and the system learned nothing.

The best part answers a complaint Junyan has had for months: he does things himself because he is faster, and then owes the system a write-up. Not anymore. Because the robot checks the real booking rather than trusting its own word, it doesn't care who did the work — Junyan moves a booking himself and the robot spots it on the next look and ticks it off. Steps the robot gets right over and over graduate to doing themselves; one rewrite sends them back to asking. Charging money, sending someone out, and making door codes never graduate, no matter how good it gets. First trial is on booking changes, with Junyan checking every step personally for the first week. Two old beliefs were corrected on the way: the booking system can be changed by a robot (our procedures wrongly said otherwise, which is why so many jobs sat waiting on staff who owed nothing), and the alarm system still can't — so we're going hunting for a way, because a renter stuck outside is worse than a robot making a door code.

August 5 (late): the thank-you messages were written on time — nobody pressed send. We went looking for a broken sweep and found the opposite. The robot ran every half hour for six days straight, found every finished booking on its own day, and wrote the thank-you within the hour — seven of them. A thank-you does not go out on its own, though: it waits at "needs a person". Those seven waited, nobody released them, and past one day they can never be sent. The ones a person did release went out in 15 to 45 minutes, so nothing further down the line is broken. The waiting list is only shown once a night, at 10:50pm — miss that night and it is never put in front of anyone again. One booking was a genuine miss: Sarra's July 31 event ended at 11:30pm, after the last check of the day, and no later check ever looks back at yesterday. Worst part: the two alarms meant to catch all this could never have gone off — one counts skipped sweeps and the sweep never skipped, the other asks a question the robot has no way to look up. We had two alarms pointed at a job that was working and none pointed at the pile where messages were actually dying. The board is clean now. Next call is Junyan's: let the robot just send the thank-you (it already auto-sends the we-missed-you text), or keep the check and nag before the day runs out.

August 5 (evening): the chores at the end of the robot's list kept getting skipped — Junyan picked the fix. Every pass, the main robot does its two big jobs first — alarms and inbox — and those grow with the day, so on busy days it runs out of room before reaching the small chores at the end. The same three chores have been pushed to "next time" on every recent pass, and nothing ever made "next time" arrive. Junyan ruled: split the work up so those chores get their own turn — and that split is already planned inside the big triage-redesign ticket, so it carries the fix rather than a new gadget being built. Until it lands, the skipping continues by choice, not by accident. A second ruling came out of the same report: Skedda's own paid marker beats a stale [Unpaid] label — four charges flagged Monday turned out already paid; the calendar label just never gets rewritten when money lands. The robots now check Skedda first before queuing a charge.

August 5: new safety checks now start on probation — and a job that changed hands quietly stopped getting done. Ten new checks are designed: does yesterday's paperwork exist, do the booking lists agree, did any booking go unbilled. After last week's false alarms, none of them get to raise a flag on day one. A new check reports quietly for five days while a person marks each finding real or noise; five clean days in a row and it earns the right to speak up on its own. The list-matching checks never earn it — two lists disagreeing is usually just one being a few minutes behind, so a person reads those every morning, permanently. The four paperwork checks go first, alone, because they are the cheapest way to prove the whole idea works.

Checking one loose end turned up something worse. Thank-you messages after a booking moved to a new robot on July 31. Seven have gone unsent since that exact day. The old pile really was cleared — this is a new one that started the moment the job moved. The desk already had a written rule saying an unsent thank-you the next day means that sweep is broken; four of these are five days past it and nobody read the line. Same shape as the July lockout, third time: naming a new owner is not the same as checking the new owner actually does it.

And it drove one more decision. Three times now a job has moved to a new robot and quietly stopped getting done. The fix goes into the master job list: every job writes down what it should produce, when we last saw it produce that, and how long is too long. Go quiet for too long and the job itself raises a fault. Right after a job changes hands we watch it twice as closely, because that is exactly when it is least proven. Four boxes and one check — deliberately small.

Junyan settled it with a house rule: past one day, a thank-you is never sent. A “how did it go” arriving days after the visit reads worse than saying nothing. So all eight get marked will-not-send, and the new check gets its shape for free — it has one day to notice, it only asks that somebody decided (sent, or will-not-send), and it must never nudge anyone into sending a late one. It also makes the missing sweep more serious than it looked: a pass that gets skipped loses that message for good. There is no catching up later. The rule is now written into the robots' own manual, so the next robot to touch this reads it without anyone remembering to say so — including a new line telling the alarm robot that marking a booking finished is now a deadline, not a chore.

August 4: the safety checker is alive again — and its first alarm was a false one. The hourly checker had been dead since July 29 (84 crashed runs, because a field it asks about was deleted from Notion) while its page kept showing the last good day. It is fixed and now lives in the cloud, not on Junyan's laptop, and files its own error row if it ever fails again. Its first report said sixteen approved messages were stuck unsent — they were not. Every one was scheduled for later, which is a feature; the sender hits its times to the minute. The checker was also flagging its own alarm rows as problems, and those rows were quietly hiding a real one. All fixed: yesterday's 27 problems are really eleven, and all eleven are real.
